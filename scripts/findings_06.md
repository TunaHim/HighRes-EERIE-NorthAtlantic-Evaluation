**What this means, in plain words**

1. **The real-world link is clear for rainfall extremes, but its strength over UK and Ireland depends on the reference.** Both references agree on the sign. E-OBS gives correlations of about 0.5 (significant); ERA5 gives about 0.33 (not quite significant with 29 winters). In western Norway both references agree on a strong link (correlations 0.74 to 0.81). The two references' response maps resemble each other well (pattern correlation 0.72 to 0.83), so the regional pattern is clear even where the UK number is uncertain.
2. **Both models reproduce the textbook response of ordinary winter rainfall** (Figure 1): wetter in the north, drier in the south in positive-NAO winters. This is the precondition for the rest.
3. **IFS-FESOM2-SR reproduces the NAO link to rainfall extremes:** right sign, correlations over UK and Ireland (0.57 and 0.60) at the E-OBS end of the observed range, a response per SD as large as observed or larger, the right sign in western Norway and north-west France, and the closest response maps to both references (pattern correlation up to 0.74 with ERA5 for R95p).
4. **ICON-ESM-ER's link over UK and Ireland is weak** (0.28 and 0.36), similar to ERA5's but below E-OBS's, so with two references it cannot be called wrong there. Two things are clearly off: its link in western Norway is weaker than in both references, and in north-west France it has the **opposite sign** to both references for very-wet-day rain (−0.46 against +0.28 to +0.34). In this model the dry side of the NAO reaches too far north (visible in Figure 1).
5. **The mildest winter day (TXx) shows a weak link over UK and Ireland in both references** (0.31 to 0.32), and a clear one over Scandinavia that neither model reproduces (pattern correlations 0.17 to 0.30). Winter TXx is the least well captured of the three.
6. **Few model grid cells pass the FDR-controlled test,** against 8–16% of land cells in each reference. A weaker NAO (standard deviation 0.52 and 0.78 of ERA5's in these winters) gives the models less signal to detect, so fewer dots is expected and is not by itself a fault.
7. **The choice of NAO index matters for ICON-ESM-ER and not for the others.** With ICON-ESM-ER's own leading pattern its UK correlations vanish (0.02 and −0.06), confirming notebook 04: that pattern is not a clean NAO. For both references and IFS-FESOM2-SR all three definitions agree.

8. **Over UK and Ireland, no correlation can be told apart from another in 29 winters** (section 10). The bootstrap 95% intervals are wide (for R95p: E-OBS 0.24 to 0.73, ERA5 0.06 to 0.61, ICON-ESM-ER 0.09 to 0.58, IFS-FESOM2-SR 0.30 to 0.80) and overlap; formal tests of each model against each reference give p = 0.2 to 1.0. The difference between the two references (0.51 against 0.35) is therefore within sampling noise as well. No single winter carries any result: leaving out the most influential winter (1989, 2009 or 2010) moves a correlation by at most 0.1. Memory between winters is negligible (effective sample 27 to 29 winters).
9. **The model differences that do pass the test are outside UK and Ireland.** ICON-ESM-ER's link is weaker than both references in western Norway (R95p 0.52 against 0.81, p = 0.04 to 0.05) and has the opposite sign in north-west France (R95p −0.46 against +0.28 to +0.34, p < 0.01). IFS-FESOM2-SR is consistent with both references in all three regions.
10. **ICON-ESM-ER's shifted circulation explains part, not all, of its wet bias** (section 11). Over 1986–2014 its average winter sits 2.1 SD along the ERA5 NAO pattern (IFS-FESOM2-SR: 0.2 SD). Multiplied by the observed NAO response, that offset would produce 38–74% of its very-wet-day rain (R95p) bias over UK and Ireland, depending on the reference, and 18–39% of its wettest-day (RX1day) bias. With the model's own NAO response the estimates rise to about 100% and 40–50%. As expected for the control, IFS-FESOM2-SR's small offset explains little of its smaller bias.
11. **Where the circulation explains the bias, and where it does not.** The expected bias is concentrated on windward coasts and mountains (western Scotland, western Norway), as a stronger westerly flow would produce. The actual bias is spread almost everywhere, including Iberia and southern France, where a positive-NAO-like circulation should make the model *drier* (Figure 12; pattern correlation between expected and actual bias only 0.1 to 0.4). So a second, broad wet bias exists that the large-scale circulation does not explain. It is largest in the single wettest day.
12. **ICON-ESM-ER rains both too often and too hard.** Over UK and Ireland land it has 61 wet days per winter against 46 in E-OBS and 43 in ERA5 (+34% to +43%), and 7.2 mm per wet day against 5.9 and 5.6 mm (+21% to +28%). IFS-FESOM2-SR is within about 10% on both. Too many wet days fits a too-strong, too-persistent westerly flow; too much rain per wet day points to the model's rain-producing processes. Both contribute, which is consistent with the circulation explaining only part of the bias.

**Answer to the question.** IFS-FESOM2-SR reproduces the sign, the regional pattern and the strength of the observed NAO link to winter rainfall extremes, and is consistent with both references in all three regions. ICON-ESM-ER gets the sign right in the north, but its response is weaker than observed in Norway and has the wrong sign in north-west France; those two differences pass formal tests. Over UK and Ireland itself, 29 winters cannot separate any dataset from another, and the two references differ from each other within sampling noise. ICON-ESM-ER's mean circulation offset is consistent with a large part of its very-wet-day rain bias, mainly on windward coasts and mountains, but less than half of its wettest-day bias and not its broad wet bias further south; the model also rains too hard on a given wet day. Neither model reproduces the link to the mildest winter day over Scandinavia. With 29 winters and one member each these are indications, not established model properties.
